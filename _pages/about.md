Hi! I study how everyday perceptions of the economy inform broader societal relations. I am generally interested in economic inequalities, state selectivities, international ordering, and business-government relations. Methodologically, my research has a strong cultural-historical dimension, and I am keen to explore questions typically considered the domain of political science using archival research methods with the aim of shedding new light on present-day developments.

These themes came together in my doctoral thesis, which I successfully defended at the University of Warwick's Department of Politics and International Studies in September 2026. My research focused on everyday perceptions of intra-state inequality and their relationship with the broader international system. Put briefly, I studied whether non-elite experiences of economic polarization within a hegemon inform its approach to international order. To explore this question, my research examined the hegemonic transition from Pax Britannica to Pax Americana, and I aimed to contribute to the literature on the outward effects of domestic intra-state dynamics, such as specific configurations of state selectivities and business-government-society relations, on hegemony and the stability of world order. The empirical side of my work was therefore heavily based on cultural-historical explorations of various archives in the U.S. and the U.K. Parts of my thesis have been published in the <a href="https://doi.org/10.1017/S0260210525101083" class="blue-color" style="font-style: italic;">Review of International Studies</a> and the <a href="https://doi.org/10.1080/09692290.2024.2401432" class="blue-color" style="font-style: italic;">Review of International Political Economy</a>.

I am currently developing these lines of research further. This includes the use of history in policymaking processes and the everyday underpinnings of national growth models.
